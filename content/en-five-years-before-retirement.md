Five years before retirement, a useful first step is to identify the decisions that remain open: when to stop working, expected spending, income sources and the role of investments. This article offers a discussion agenda, not a conclusion about whether you can afford to retire.

## 1. Put your retirement date in context

Write down the date you have in mind and what could change it. Would you prefer to leave work at once or reduce your hours gradually? Does the plan depend on a family event, a sale or an employer’s decision? Separate preferences from constraints you already know about.

The Financial Consumer Agency of Canada recommends considering lifestyle, spending and timing when preparing for retirement. [Explore its retirement-planning resources](https://www.canada.ca/en/financial-consumer-agency/services/retirement-planning.html).

## 2. Turn an impression into a budget discussion

Make three columns: current expenses, costs that might disappear and new expenses to consider. Add a line for one-time plans. You do not need a perfect number immediately. The purpose is to make your assumptions visible.

Hypothetical example: someone expects fewer commuting costs but plans to travel more. Saying that spending “will go down” does not fully describe that person’s plan. This is not a client story and does not suggest a particular outcome.

## 3. List the information you have

Note your plans and accounts, along with documents you still need to obtain. Distinguish an estimate from confirmed income. Retraite Québec offers preparation and planning tools; read their explanations to understand what a result includes and leaves out. [View Retraite Québec’s tools (French)](https://www.retraitequebec.gouv.qc.ca/fr/services-ligne-et-outils/seances-information-et-outils-planification).

Do not send these documents through the public website form. If you begin a professional relationship, agree on an appropriate way to share them.

## 4. Prepare questions about your investments

Which amounts might you need soon? Which investments would you like explained? Which costs do you want to understand? What would you revisit if your retirement date changed? Write down the points you are unsure about, too. An explicit uncertainty is more useful than a guessed answer.

## 5. Create an agenda you can use

Choose the three questions that matter most to you. For each, identify the missing information and whom to ask. This list can be a starting point for a conversation with Bill. Agree directly on the available services, any analysis and their terms.

Preparing this agenda does not validate a withdrawal amount or retirement date. A personal decision requires a closer look at your circumstances and, where appropriate, personal tax or legal advice.
